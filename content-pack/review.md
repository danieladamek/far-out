# FAR Out: every route into federal work for a small business

<!-- section: abstract -->
## Abstract

The United States government buys through several systems, not one, and the route a small business enters by decides the rules it lives under. This guide maps those routes for a firm with no prior federal work. Four families cover the ground: direct contracts, where set-asides and sole-source awards reserve work for small firms and the certified programs under Part 19 of the Federal Acquisition Regulation, and where the GSA Schedule and the governmentwide vehicles carry much of the ordering [384] [418]; research funding, where SBIR and STTR reserve 3.2 and 0.45 percent of the large agencies' extramural research budgets for small firms, reauthorized on April 13, 2026 to September 30, 2031 after a lapse [3] [1]; agreements outside the FAR, where Other Transactions and Commercial Solutions Openings buy prototypes and commercial solutions under their own statutes, mostly through consortia [1028] [1053] [1121]; and work through others, where subcontracting plans, teaming, joint ventures and mentor-protégé arrangements put a small firm on a larger firm's contract under rules about who counts as small and who gets paid when [692] [770].

Each route carries the gates a firm must clear first, from registration in SAM.gov to the cybersecurity and accounting rules that switch on by contract type [303] [625], and each is read against the government's side of the table: the six defense acquisition pathways and the civilian and grants models [1237] [1324]. A dated ledger records what moved in 2025 and 2026, because much of it did: the FAR rewrite now in force by deviation beside the codified text, the thresholds that rose on October 1, 2025, the 8(a) rule that changed on September 10, 2026, the CMMC phase suspended on July 13, 2026 [386] [340] [314] [625]. Where official sources disagree, both figures are given. Nothing here is advice, and nothing is tailored to any firm. The sweep closed on October 3, 2026, and 1,318 of the 1,324 cited sources were read against their live pages before publication.

## Why this matters: several systems, not one

A first look at federal contracting suggests one enormous customer with one rulebook. It is closer to the truth to say there are several customers with several rulebooks, and that the door a firm walks through decides which one applies. A firm that wins a set-aside contract lives under the Federal Acquisition Regulation, with its representations, clauses and thresholds; a firm that wins a prototype agreement from a defense consortium does not, and the department's own guide says the FAR, the Cost Accounting Standards and the Contract Disputes Act are not applicable to such an agreement [1053] [1063]. The same firm, with the same product, can be a contractor, an awardee, a subcontractor or a consortium member, and each word carries a different set of obligations and protections.

The money is real and the share reserved for small firms is set in law. The statute sets a governmentwide goal of not less than 23 percent of prime contract dollars for small businesses, with 5 percent each for small disadvantaged, women-owned and service-disabled veteran-owned firms and 3 percent for HUBZone firms [374]. SBA's release for fiscal year 2025 reports nearly 28 percent of prime contract dollars, $179 billion, going to small businesses [382]. The research side is reserved too: agencies with large extramural research budgets must spend not less than 3.2 percent on SBIR and 0.45 percent on STTR, programs only small firms may hold [3] [10]. Outside the FAR, defense obligations through Other Transactions passed $18 billion in fiscal year 2024, about half of the prototype money moving through consortia [1121].

The reservation works through mechanics that are easy to miss. Under the rule of two, a purchase between the micro-purchase threshold and the simplified acquisition threshold is set aside for small business unless the contracting officer cannot expect two competitive offers, and the same presumption applies above that threshold when two responsible small firms are expected [384]. Those thresholds moved on October 1, 2025, to $15,000 and $350,000 [417] [340]. A firm that does not know the band exists cannot see why some solicitations are closed to large firms and some are not.

Timing matters as much as the rules, and the official pages lag. The FAR itself is being rewritten: an April 2025 executive order directed the cut, agencies now follow rewritten model text by class deviation while the codified text remains in force, and the first proposed rules appeared in June and September 2026 with nothing yet final [386] [403] [418]. SBIR and STTR lapsed for six months before the April 2026 reauthorization, and the SBA directive that implements them is still the May 2023 version [1] [7]. The 8(a) eligibility rule changed on September 10, 2026 [314]. The defense cybersecurity certification, CMMC, took effect as a rule in November 2025 and had its second phase suspended on July 13, 2026 [625]. A guide written a year ago would be wrong on each of these points, and so are many agency pages read today.

Taken together, these facts set the shape of this guide: routes rather than rules, each route with its gates, and a dated record of what changed, so that a reader can tell current law from deviation from proposal. <!-- synthesis -->
The chapters that follow move from the buyer's side of the table to the firm's: how the government buys, who the buyers are, what it means to be small, how research gets funded, how work comes through others, what the gates are, and what moved.

## How the government buys: FAR and non-FAR

Taken together, the rules below describe a sequence: an agency plans a purchase, forecasts it, researches the market, and only then picks a buying method [1323] [579] [1324]. <!-- synthesis -->
Most methods sit in the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR); a few defense instruments rest on statutes outside it, and a rewritten FAR is now in use beside the codified one [1053] [400].

**The people.** The contracting officer decides whether a purchase is set aside and assigns the NAICS code that fixes the size standard [384] [285]. DoD's business-systems instruction tells program managers to engage industry through market research [1258]. The agency's small business specialist is consulted no later than 30 days before a solicitation [428]. SBA's procurement center representative recommends set-asides within 15 days of receiving an acquisition package [430].

**From plan to award.** FAR Part 7 requires planning and market research for every acquisition, and Part 10 puts that research before soliciting above the simplified acquisition threshold [1323] [1324]. Agencies with more than $50 million in annual contract actions must prepare a forecast for small businesses; GSA's governmentwide forecast tool needs no registration [579] [582]. A sources-sought notice is, per GSA, "primarily used to identify small businesses capable of performing/providing the requirement" [572].

Proposed actions over $25,000 are synopsized at SAM.gov, the governmentwide point of entry [562] [418]. Outside commercial buys, the synopsis appears at least 15 days before the solicitation, and above the simplified acquisition threshold offerors get at least 30 days to respond [563].

**The methods.** Three thresholds, in force since October 1, 2025, sort most purchases; Part 13 supplies simplified procedures up to the second [417] [438].

| Threshold | Amount | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Micro-purchase | $15,000 | Purchase card is the preferred method [417] [440] |
| Simplified acquisition | $350,000 | Between $15,000 and $350,000, set aside for small business [417] [438] |
| Commercial simplified procedures | $9 million ($15 million for emergency categories) | Commercial buys only [417] [439] |

Under Part 19's rule of two, a purchase between the first two thresholds is set aside unless two competitive small-business offers cannot reasonably be expected, and statute reserves the same band [384] [374]. Above $350,000 an acquisition is set aside when offers are expected from at least two responsible small businesses at fair market prices; the 8(a), HUBZone, SDVOSB and WOSB programs are considered first, in no order of precedence [384] [429].

Part 12 tells agencies to buy commercial products and services when they meet the need, and market research is what sends a buy there [442] [1324]. Part 15 governs negotiated buys, with exchanges with industry before the solicitation [1325]. Pending: a proposed rule of September 18, 2026 would move micro-purchase procedures into Part 12 [405].

**Outside the FAR.** An other transaction (OT) is a legally binding instrument that is not a standard procurement contract, grant or cooperative agreement. The FAR does not automatically apply; the Procurement Integrity Act and fiscal law do [1053]. A research OT (10 U.S.C. 4021) expects other parties to match Government funds where practicable and carries no follow-on production authority [1031] [1055]. A prototype OT (10 U.S.C. 4022) needs one of four conditions: a nontraditional defense contractor or nonprofit research institution participating to a significant extent; all significant participants being small businesses or nontraditional contractors; one third of the cost from non-Federal sources; or a written finding of exceptional circumstances [1028].

A commercial solutions opening (CSO) is a general solicitation under 10 U.S.C. 3458. Before section 1823 of Pub. L. 119-60 (December 18, 2025) the statute covered only "innovative" commercial products and services [1034]. The amendment dropped that limit and added nondevelopmental items and follow-on production [1033] [806]. DFARS Subpart 212.70 and DoD's Part 212 class deviation still say "innovative" [1040] [1046]. A CSO may award prototype OTs under section 4022 or FAR-based contracts under section 3458 [1058]. A broad agency announcement (FAR 35.016) covers basic and applied research not tied to a specific system; DoD says it "Cannot be used for production" [271] [1057].

Consortia carry much of the OT money: by GAO's count, nearly two-thirds of DoD's prototype OT dollars in fiscal years 2019–2021 and about half of prototype OT obligations in 2021–2024 [1071] [1121]. DoD awards a base agreement to a management organization and selects the member, and the manager issues a sub-award; the route record for consortium membership lists dues. For most consortia, GAO reports, "members pay dues to join the consortium and gain exclusive access to government opportunities" [1119].

**The overhaul.** Executive Order 14275 (April 15, 2025) told the FAR Council to cut the FAR to what statute requires or is otherwise necessary [386]. Agencies adopt the rewritten model text by class deviation: GSA took up Part 19 effective November 3, 2025, DoD effective February 1, 2026 [400] [402]. The codified FAR (FAC 2026-01, effective 03/13/2026) is unchanged, and this chapter cites its numbering [418]. Twelve proposed rules are planned; batches appeared on June 23 and September 18, 2026, and a Federal Register search on October 3, 2026 returned no final rule [403] [410] [407]. The sunset is disputed: the order and model section 1.109 expire non-statutory sections after four years unless renewed [386] [392], while the June 2026 proposed rule's preamble says "Sections, provisions and clauses do not expire until removed from the Code of Federal Regulations through rulemaking" [403].

## The buyer's map: the AAF pathways, the civilian model, and the 2025–26 reforms

The federal government buys in three broad ways. Defense sorts what it buys into the six pathways of the Adaptive Acquisition Framework (AAF), each with its own instruction and clock. Civilian agencies buy under the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR). Grants fund a public purpose instead of buying for the government's own use. What follows records what each text says about small-firm entry [1237] [1323] [1017] [1018].

On the defense side, a program manager recommends a pathway and a decision authority approves it; pathways may be combined [1239].

**Urgent Capability Acquisition** fields urgent needs "in less than 2 years". Its instruction, DoDI 5000.81 (December 2019), was not read. The pathway's contracting page names Federal Supply Schedules, letter contracts, orders under existing contracts and three other-transaction-family authorities [1243] [1238] [1244].

**Middle Tier of Acquisition** (10 U.S.C. 3602; DoDI 5000.80) has two paths: rapid prototyping, with a residual operational capability within 5 years, and rapid fielding, with production beginning within 6 months. The instruction (December 30, 2019 as returned; November 2024 on the official policy list) has no industry or small-business language. The overview page names other transactions and procurement for experimental purposes as ways small and nontraditional firms compete [1249] [1248] [1238] [1246].

**Major Capability Acquisition** (DoDI 5000.85, listed as November 2021) runs through Milestones A, B and C. Its acquisition strategy must reflect a small business strategy [1250] [1251] [1238].

**Software Acquisition** (DoDI 5000.87, October 2020) requires first releases (MVP and MVCR) within 1 year of first obligation, then deliveries at least annually. The November 2025 strategy, restating an unread March 2025 memorandum, makes commercial solutions openings (CSOs) and other transactions the default way to solicit and award here [1254] [1238] [1264].

**Defense Business Systems** (DoDI 5000.75, listed as January 2020) follows the five-phase Business Capability Acquisition Cycle. Program managers must engage industry through market research, requests for information and industry days [1256] [1258] [1238].

**Acquisition of Services** (DoDI 5000.74, listed as June 2021) applies to contracted services at or above the simplified acquisition threshold. The instruction tells components to increase small business participation as primes and subcontractors [1259] [1261] [1238].

Two reform layers now sit over these instructions. Policy came first: the Department announced three memoranda and an Acquisition Transformation Strategy on November 7, 2025. The strategy gives Portfolio Acquisition Executives (PAEs) expanded authorities, directs other transaction authority for prototype and follow-on production, and delegates Middle Tier approvals. It aims to "broaden vendor participation to lower barriers for small businesses and startups" [1263] [1264].

The lead memorandum, on the Warfighting Acquisition System, was announced the same day but could not be read. Its content is pending confirmation: digests report four-year PAE terms, a commercial-first preference and deadlines on which they differ [1263] [1265] [1266] [1267].

Statute followed. The FY2026 NDAA (Pub. L. 119-60, December 18, 2025) put the PAE in 10 U.S.C. 1732: program managers report to the PAE, who is to "prioritize the use of transactions for prototype projects". The Act also removed the definition of innovative from the defense CSO statute and added follow-on production authority [806] [1273] [1033].

Two enacted provisions are pending here, their text unread and known only by heading and digest: section 1822 (reported as a written memorandum before noncommercial methods) and section 824 (reported as nongovernment past performance references) [806] [1108] [1274].

The framework instruction is listed as DoWI 5000.02, April 2026. The text retrieved was still the January 23, 2020 version as changed June 8, 2022; a secondary site reports an 8 April 2026 change. What it says is unread [1238] [1241] [1242].

Taken together, the civilian texts read here show no set of pathways comparable to the AAF; the FAR's parts do that work. <!-- synthesis -->
Parts 7 and 10 require planning and market research for all acquisitions, and Part 12 procedures apply when commercial items can meet the need. Under Part 13, purchases above the micro-purchase threshold ($15,000) and at or below the simplified acquisition threshold ($350,000) "shall be set aside for small business concerns". Part 15 allows exchanges with industry before the request for proposals [1323] [1324] [602] [418] [417] [1325].

Each agency has an Office of Small and Disadvantaged Business Utilization (OSDBU) whose director reports to the agency head or deputy. For specified large or consolidated buys, the package goes to SBA's procurement center representative (PCR) at least 30 days before solicitation; the PCR recommends within 15 days [1326] [374] [1327].

Under 15 U.S.C. 637(a)(12)(C), agencies reporting contract actions above $50,000,000 prepare a forecast, and "such forecasts shall be available to small business concerns". Acquisition.gov indexes 22 agency forecast pages, each with a buyer record [579] [581].

Grants are the third model. Under 31 U.S.C. 6303–6305, a procurement contract is the instrument when the principal purpose is to acquire property or services "for the direct benefit or use of the United States Government". A grant transfers a thing of value for a public purpose where substantial agency involvement is not expected; a cooperative agreement is used where it is. Under 2 CFR Part 200, notices of funding opportunity are posted on Grants.gov and generally stay open at least 60 calendar days [1017] [1018] [1019] [1328].

Taken together, small-business entry is explicit in only a few of these texts: the Major Capability and Services instructions, the Middle Tier overview page, the strategy, and FAR Parts 13 and 19. <!-- synthesis -->
The Middle Tier and framework instructions, as returned, are silent [1251] [1261] [1246] [1264] [602] [1326] [1248] [1241].

## Being small: size standards, affiliation, certifications

Small, in federal contracting, is a measured status. A firm is small when it sits under the ceiling for the industry a purchase is assigned to, with the firms it is tied to counted in. The certifications sit on top of that test, each with its own rules. [278] [297]

The ceiling is set per NAICS industry, in employees or in average annual receipts (13 CFR 121.201). Receipts are averaged over five completed fiscal years; employees over the preceding 24 calendar months. SBA's Size Standards Tool is at sba.gov/size-standards. [284] [281] [282] [280]

The contracting officer designates the NAICS code for each solicitation. Size is fixed at the initial offer that includes price and generally lasts for the contract. 13 CFR 125.12 qualifies that: recertification is required within 30 calendar days of a merger, acquisition or sale that changes controlling interest, and on contracts longer than five years. Its paragraph (g) uses January 17, 2026 as the transition date. [285] [290] [291]

Pending: a proposed rule of August 20, 2026 (91 FR 53741) would collapse nearly 1,000 size standards into 338 and proposes to reduce none. Comments closed September 21, 2026; there is no effective date. It does not mention the August 22, 2025 proposal to raise monetary standards for 263 industries, whose outcome was not found. [288] [287]

Affiliation decides whose numbers count. SBA's page states: "Affiliation with another business is based on the power to control, whether exercised or not." Affiliates' employees and receipts are added to the firm's own. 13 CFR 121.103(b) lists exceptions, among them firms owned by Indian Tribes, Alaska Native Corporations, Native Hawaiian Organizations and Community Development Corporations, and SBA-approved mentor-protégé relationships; the affiliation record carries the rest. [278] [297]

SAM registration comes first. FAR 52.204-7 reads: "An Offeror is required to be registered in SAM when submitting an offer or quotation and at time of award." Registration is free and is renewed every 365 days. [303] [301]

**8(a)** is a nine-year, one-time business development program for small firms at least 51 percent owned and controlled by socially and economically disadvantaged individuals. Final rule 91 FR 51568, effective September 10, 2026, removed the rebuttable presumption for individually owned applicants and applies to applications pending on that date; it says it does not affect entity-owned firms. An applicant now shows discrimination against a group to which the individual belongs, and material harm. SBA returned pending individually owned applications that day, with 45 calendar days to resubmit. [310] [311] [314] [312] [325]

The program is also under audit: SBA announced 1,091 firms suspended on January 28, 2026 and, on March 4, 2026, termination proceedings against 628 firms, nearly 800 in total. [321] [323]

On the sole-source ceiling the official texts disagree. The codified FAR allows an 8(a) award without competition up to $8.5 million for manufacturing NAICS codes and $5.5 million otherwise. 13 CFR 124.506 prints $7,000,000 and $4,500,000, and SBA's program page, rechecked on October 3, 2026, gave $7 million and $4.5 million. The justification line differs too: $30 million in FAR 19.808-1, $25,000,000 in 13 CFR 124.506. For HUBZone and for WOSB and EDWOSB the codified FAR gives $8.5 million and $5.5 million; for SDVOSB, $8.5 million and $5 million. [338] [331] [310] [339] [349] [357] [368]

**HUBZone** certification requires a principal office in a HUBZone and at least 35% of employees living in one. It carries a 10 percent price evaluation preference against large-business offers and is recertified every three years. [341] [343] [347] [433] [345]

**WOSB and EDWOSB** certification is for small firms at least 51% owned and controlled by women who are U.S. citizens. Set-asides exist only in designated industries: SBA's list, effective October 1, 2022, has 733. Certification is free through SBA or comes from one of four approved third-party certifiers, with a program examination every three years. [351] [358] [353]

**VOSB and SDVOSB** certification moved from VA to SBA on January 1, 2023 and runs three years; SDVOSBs may no longer self-certify for awards that count toward SDVOSB goals. Inside VA, Veterans First ranks SDVOSBs first and VOSBs second, and sets a purchase aside when two or more certified firms are expected at a fair and reasonable price. [361] [366] [367] [369]

**Small disadvantaged business** status has no application: a firm represents it in good faith, and all current 8(a) participants qualify. The statutory goals are not less than 23 percent of prime contract value for small business, 5 percent each for disadvantaged, women-owned and service-disabled veteran-owned firms, and 3 percent for HUBZone firms; SBA's goaling page gives the same figures. SBA wrote in February 2025: "SBA has returned the 8(a) SDB contracting goal to its statutory level of 5%." Its FY2025 scorecard release reports nearly 28% of prime contracts, $179 billion, to small businesses, 11.6% to SDBs, and a government-wide grade of A. [372] [374] [375] [317] [382]

Size protests, and HUBZone, WOSB and SDVOSB status protests, are due by the fifth business day after bid opening or notice of the apparent successful offeror; 8(a) eligibility may not be challenged by another participant. For willful misrepresentation of size, 13 CFR 121.108 and 15 U.S.C. 632(w) set "a presumption of loss to the United States based on the total amount expended", and bidding on a set-aside is itself a certification. [294] [458] [462] [464] [463] [337] [295] [296]

## Getting funded for R&D: SBIR/STTR end to end

SBIR and STTR pay small firms to research and develop their own technology, in phases. Only small firms can hold the awards, and large research agencies must spend 3.2 percent or more (SBIR) and 0.45 percent (STTR) of extramural research budgets on them, which makes this the route built for a small firm with a technology [10][3]. Eleven agencies take part, six with STTR, each differently [12].

The authority is 15 U.S.C. 638 and the SBA Policy Directive effective May 3, 2023 [3][5]. The codified statute ended SBIR authority on September 30, 2025; NIH's notice dates the expiry to October 1, 2025 [3][27]. Pub. L. 119-83, approved April 13, 2026, restored the programs through September 30, 2031, after what the Congressional Research Service calls a six-month lapse [1][59].

The act adds a tier: a Strategic Breakthrough award is not more than $30,000,000 over not more than 48 months, with not less than 100 percent matching funds [1]. From fiscal year 2027 each agency sets its own cap on proposals per firm; two were verified, nine Phase I, Fast-track and Direct to Phase II applications per fiscal year across HHS and one proposal per organization under NSF 26-510 [1][29][35]. Agencies may deny an award on security risk, including ties to entities on eight named lists; a denial does not bar a later cycle [1]. Direct to Phase II runs until September 30, 2031 and now covers DOE, NASA and STTR, which the posted directive does not show [1][5]. Assistance funding is capped at $6,500 (Phase I) and $50,000 (Phase II) per project [1].

A firm, with its affiliates, must have not more than 500 employees and be more than 50 percent owned and controlled by U.S. citizens, permanent resident aliens or other owners the size rule lists; only SBIR admits firms majority-owned by multiple venture capital, hedge or private equity funds [10]. STTR requires not less than 40 percent of the work by the firm and 30 percent by a research institution [5]. Base guidelines are $150,000 for Phase I and $1,000,000 for Phase II; as of April 2026 agencies may go to $323,090 and $2,153,927 without SBA approval [5][11].

The defense department (Department of War, DoW, on its pages) accepts proposals only through its DSIP portal [12][52]. Its opportunities page still describes three scheduled releases a year; AFWERX's April 2026 briefing lists six for FY2026 [52][41]. Amounts vary by component: AFWERX up to $323,000 and $2.1 million and DARPA typically $250K and $1.8M [64][86]. The Army's page gives Phase I up to $300,000; SBA's tutorial gives up to $111,500 with a $56,000 option [81][57].

The program records carry dates and steps. NIH takes grant applications under parent notices PA-27-100 (SBIR) and PA-27-102 (STTR), due September 5, January 5 and April 5 [135][137]. NSF requires a Project Pitch first and funds up to $305,000 (Phase I) and $1,250,000 (Phase II) [35]. DOE invites full applications only after a successful pitch [161]. NASA awards contracts, per SBA's card, through appendices to a broad agency announcement, with proposals in ProSAMS [12][168][169]. USDA's FY 2026 Phase II closes November 17, 2026 and EPA's solicitation November 8, 2026 [183][201]. DOT's FY 2026 solicitation closed July 7, 2026 and Education's June 29, 2026 [191][206]. NIST has no active funding notice; NOAA's FY27 notice is to open in Fall 2026 [208][214]. DHS is unverified: its page limits Phase I to $100,000, SBA's card says $175,000, and its portal was not read [196][12].

Phase III is work that derives from, extends or completes earlier SBIR/STTR work, paid from other funds [5]. Under 15 U.S.C. 638(r)(4) an agency may award it sole source without a FAR Part 6 justification or synopsis, and the directive sets no limit on number, duration, type or dollar value and lifts the Phase I and II size limits [225][230][5]. FAR 6.302-5 does not mention Phase III; a rule proposed in 2023 (FAR Case 2020-010) would amend Part 6 [226][228]. The act's Phase III measures are pending: contracting-officer training, model contracts, and a directive revision due April 13, 2027, while SBIR.gov still posts the May 2023 directive [1][7].

Taken together, the uncapped phase is where the programs can be worth most to a firm, yet GAO's 2025 report found Phase III data "not reliable enough" to measure it [5][242]. <!-- synthesis -->

Award data is protected for 20 years from award under the directive and DFARS 252.227-7018, but FAR 52.227-20 (May 2014) still says 4 years after acceptance of all items; that proposed rule would change 4 to 20 [5][24][25][228].

Defense programs add money after Phase II. AFWERX's TACFI ($375,000–$2 million) and STRATFI ($3–15 million) need matching funds and a government submitter [64][66]. Army CATALYST matches Army SBIR, Army customer and integrator funds 2:1:1; the Navy's STP is an 11-month assistance program [239][235]. ART matches a DoW sponsor's funds 1:1 under a Technology Transition Agreement [55]. APFIT is procurement funding, $10 million to $50 million per project, that only a government organization can request [108][109].

Beside SBIR, a broad agency announcement under FAR 35.016 selects research proposals by peer or scientific review [271]. A CRADA is joint work in which a federal laboratory may give resources but not funds [992]. Agencies now run prize competitions themselves: Challenge.gov was sunset on March 30, 2026, and GSA points the public to USA.gov/challenges [276][275].

<!-- section: 6-working-through-others -->
## Working through others: subcontracting, teaming, joint ventures, mentor-protégé, consortia

A small firm can do federal work without holding a federal contract.
Taken together, the rules below show each form answering three questions differently: who counts as small, who does the work, who gets paid when. <!-- synthesis -->
Subcontractors, SBA says, "do not work directly with the government, but instead work for other contractors" [730].

A prime that is not small must hold a subcontracting plan on a contract expected to exceed $900,000 ($2 million for construction), up from $750,000 and $1.5 million since October 1, 2025 [692] [340]; 13 CFR 125.3 still prints $750,000 and $1,500,000 [704]. Missing a goal is not by itself a failure of good faith; a willful or intentional failure is a material breach carrying liquidated damages [692] [694] [437]. The prime must make a good faith effort to use the small firms it bid with, or explain in writing within 30 days of contract completion [436].

Plan reports are filed in Subcontracting Plan Reporting on SAM.gov; eSRS was decommissioned on February 20, 2026 [721] [720]. First-tier subcontract reporting moved to SAM.gov when FSRS.gov retired on March 8, 2025 [696] [724].

SubNet lists opportunities posted by large contractors, and on October 3, 2026 was live with current listings on legacy.sba.gov [443] [731]. SBA's prime directory files run FY 2018 to FY 2024; GSA's file name is dated March 13, 2025 [732] [739]. Commercial Market Representatives are reached at subcontracting@sba.gov [733]. Primes' forms ask variously for UEI, CAGE and NAICS codes, size status, clearances, quality certifications and a capability statement [846] [856] [888]; Lockheed Martin's page says "Most opportunities will be by invitation to participate in a competitive bid" [833].

FAR subpart 9.6 recognises a joint venture acting as prime, or a prime with agreed subcontractors, if disclosed in the offer; the prime stays responsible [761]. On GSA Schedules each team member holds its own contract and is prime for its share, all must be small for a set-aside order, and GSA says subpart 9.6 does not apply [762].

The risk in such a team on a set-aside is the ostensible subcontractor: one not similarly situated that performs the primary and vital requirements, or on which the prime is unusually reliant; since January 16, 2025 the offeror is then ineligible as small [706] [292]. Outside general construction, meeting the limitations on subcontracting avoids the finding (see the gate record) [706].

A joint venture must be in writing, under its own name and identified in SAM (13 CFR 121.103(h)); it is small if each partner is small, or if a small protégé joins its SBA-approved mentor [770]. It generally may not win contracts beyond two years from its first award [770]. A mentor-protégé venture's agreement needs twelve provisions (13 CFR 125.8), with the protégé as managing venturer owning at least 51% and performing at least 40% of the work [773]. A small partner may cite the venture's contracts as past performance, except work done exclusively by other partners (13 CFR 125.11) [781].

SBA's Mentor-Protégé Program lets a for-profit mentor of any size assist a small protégé for up to six years and take up to 40% equity in it [787] [785]. Applications go through certify.sba.gov; SBA quotes 105 days [788]. 13 CFR 125.9 was last amended by a December 17, 2024 rule; no 2025–26 amendment was found [785].

DoD's program rests on 10 U.S.C. 4902: a mentor qualifies with $25,000,000 in prior-year DoD contracts, terms run not more than three years, and the mentor is reimbursed up to $1,000,000 per protégé per fiscal year or credited toward its subcontracting goals [451]. DFARS Appendix I still reads "Mentors incurring costs through September 30, 2026", though the statute is permanent [450] [451].

Other agencies' programs need SBA approval [786]. Live pages were read for NASA, DOE, DOT, Treasury and FAA [809] [813] [819] [816] [822]; GSA's ended April 3, 2019, VA's subpart is reserved, and DHS "will not accept new applications until further notice" [815] [823] [901].

For other transactions, GAO reports, "For most consortia, members pay dues to join the consortium and gain exclusive access to government opportunities" [1119]. Posted dues run from nothing to $10,000 a year; some consortia assess 0.05% to 0.5% of awarded funding [1193] [1138] [1194] [1169]. GAO found manager compensation of 0.7% to 21.3% of obligated dollars, and FPDS naming the manager "rather than the awardees performing the work" [1119] [1121].

Under FAR 52.232-40 a prime paid early pays small-business subcontractors "within 15 days after receipt of accelerated payments from the Government" [661]. A prime under a plan must report late payment: more than 30 days past due in the statute, more than 90 in FAR 52.242-5 and 13 CFR 125.3 [953] [698] [704]; SBA's proposal of 30 (December 19, 2024) has no final rule found [714]. Miller Act payment bonds on construction are required above $150,000 in the FAR, $100,000 in the statute as read [644] [947].

CPARS says "Evaluations are not completed for subcontractors." [985], but a small first-tier subcontractor under a plan may ask its prime for a rating within 30 days after performance ends, due within 15 calendar days [781]. A subcontractor has no contract with the Government, so Contract Disputes Act claims pass through the prime [976] [979], and GAO "will not consider a protest of the award or proposed award of a subcontract" [467].

## The gates: what stands between a firm and an award

A gate is anything a firm needs in place before the government can award to it or pay it: a registration, a statement about itself, or a rule that switches on with a contract's size or subject.
Taken together, the gate records sort four ways: nearly every route, above a dollar threshold, by what the work touches, and by contract type. <!-- synthesis -->
Two dates run through them: the thresholds moved on October 1, 2025, and the defense cybersecurity certification rule, CMMC, has been paused since July 13, 2026 [340] [625].

**On nearly every route.** A firm is registered in the System for Award Management (SAM.gov) at offer and again at award [303]. Registration generates the Unique Entity ID, which replaced the DUNS number on April 4, 2022; it can take up to 10 business days to become active and is renewed every 365 days [608] [300]. GSA's checklist says registration is free and that a CAGE code is assigned after submission if the firm has none; the code is needed before award above the micro-purchase threshold [301] [611].

The contracting officer puts a NAICS code and its size standard in the solicitation, and the firm represents in good faith whether it is small; size is fixed on the date of the self-certification submitted with the initial offer that includes price [613] [427] [610]. Other representations and certifications sit in the SAM record; an offer verifies that they were entered or updated within the last 12 months and are current, accurate and complete [613]. Pending: a proposed rule of June 23, 2026 (FAR Case 2026-001) would remove that provision, FAR 52.204-8, in favour of entity-level SAM representations [403].

**Above a dollar threshold.** The inflation adjustment effective October 1, 2025 raised the micro-purchase, simplified acquisition, subcontracting plan and cost-or-pricing-data figures and left the bond and wage-rate thresholds alone [340].

**By what the work touches.** The Section 889 clause bars delivering or using covered telecommunications and video surveillance equipment from five named Chinese companies; another bars articles and sources named in FASCSA orders, looked up in SAM.gov [615] [616]. Any contractor system holding Federal contract information meets the 15 basic safeguarding requirements of FAR 52.204-21 [617]. DoD adds NIST SP 800-171 with 72-hour incident reporting (DFARS 252.204-7012) and an assessment score in SPRS not more than 3 years old (252.204-7019, -7020) [618] [619] [620].

CMMC, the rule (detail is in the CMMC gate record): 32 CFR Part 170 sets three levels and four phases, each a year after the last; the DFARS rule took effect November 10, 2025 and applies at program managers' discretion through November 9, 2028 [624] [623] [622].

CMMC, the suspension: on July 13, 2026 the Department of War announced the immediate suspension of the Phase II requirements; implementation is paused in Phase 1, and "All Phase I self-assessment requirements remain firmly in place." [625] The implementing memorandum limits new procurements to Level 1 (Self) or Level 2 (Self) and keeps the NIST SP 800-171 and 252.204-7012 obligations [627]. Reported only by secondary sources: class deviation revisions of July 16 and September 3, 2026, and a review closed September 11, 2026 with no report; DoD's deviations page as fetched shows no revisions [630] [631] [401].

**By contract type.** A cost-reimbursement contract requires an adequate accounting system, judged against the SF 1408 criteria in a pre-award survey requested by a federal entity; DCAA says "There is no such thing as a DCAA approved government accounting system." [635] [636] [634] Access to classified information runs through a DCSA facility clearance, sponsored by a government contracting activity or a cleared contractor: "Contractors cannot sponsor themselves for an FCL." [637] A manufacturer of defense articles registers under the ITAR even if it never exports, at $3,000 a year in Tier 1 [640]. Service Contract Labor Standards apply to service contracts over $2,500 [650]. Construction Wage Rate Requirements apply to construction in excess of $2,000 [651]. Buy American asks 65% domestic content for 2024–2028, and trade agreements apply from $174,000 for supplies and services [652] [653].

**Getting paid.** Payment is due on the later of the 30th day after a proper invoice or after acceptance; late payment earns interest automatically, at 4-3/4 percent for July 1 through December 31, 2026 [941] [945]. DoD takes invoices through Wide Area WorkFlow, which its OT guide places in PIEE [665] [1049]. Civilian agencies use Treasury's Invoice Processing Platform, provided at no cost to vendors [664].

**Other transactions.** DARPA lists the FAR and DFARS, Cost Accounting Standards, the Truth in Negotiations Act and the Contract Disputes Act as not applicable to an OT [1063]. What remains: full SAM registration before award, payable milestones set in the agreement, and, on DoD prototype agreements over $5,000,000, a Comptroller General access clause plus DoD access to records where the agreement is cost-type [1049] [1029] [1037].

## How to read this material, and how this guide was built

Federal contracting has an unusually clear hierarchy of sources, and knowing it is most of the skill. Statute comes first: Title 15 of the United States Code for the Small Business Act and SBIR, Title 10 for defense acquisition, Title 41 for the civilian side. Regulation implements statute: the Federal Acquisition Regulation at Title 48, the Defense supplement after it, and SBA's own rules at 13 CFR Parts 121 through 128. Policy directives, class deviations and agency pages sit below both, and they are where the stale figures live [418] [10] [7].

The pages that are authoritative for a number are not always the ones that rank first in a search. For a FAR threshold, acquisition.gov shows the codified text and its Federal Acquisition Circular number; the rule that changed the thresholds in 2025 is a Federal Register document with its own citation [418] [340]. For an SBA program, the eCFR text of 13 CFR governs, and SBA's program pages can print older figures than the FAR: the 8(a) sole-source ceiling is one such case, where the FAR reads $8.5 million and $5.5 million while 13 CFR 124.506 and SBA's page read $7 million and $4.5 million [338] [331] [310]. For SBIR, the statute and the SBA Policy Directive govern, and the directive posted on SBIR.gov predates the 2026 act [3] [5] [7].

Three habits keep a reader out of trouble.
<!-- framing -->
First, check the date on the page and the date of the rule it describes; a thresholds page that still says $250,000 for the simplified acquisition threshold is describing the world before October 1, 2025 [417]. Second, when a figure matters, read it in the governing text rather than the summary: the overhaul's model text and the codified FAR now coexist, and a solicitation from an agency that adopted the deviations will use the new section numbers [400] [402]. Third, treat law-firm and trade commentary as a pointer to a primary source, never as the source; this guide follows that rule throughout, and marks the few places where only commentary reports a change [1108].

How this guide was built is on the methods page. The sweep ran on October 3, 2026, as 674 logged searches across four route families, keeping official government pages, statutes, regulations, policy directives, solicitations, oversight reports, and a company's or consortium's own page for what it says about itself. Every reference carries the date it was read. After assembly, each cited source was read again against its live page with a strict confirm-or-deny check; quotations that were not verbatim were removed, facts the page contradicted are marked in the reference and listed in the open-items file, and facts the page did not show are marked unconfirmed. Six sources could not be reached by an automated reader and are marked as such.
<!-- framing -->

The limits are stated rather than hidden. Pages were read through an automated fetch that returns extracted text, so long regulations and PDFs were sometimes read in part, and a handful of primary documents could not be read at all: the April 2026 change to the defense framework instruction, the November 2025 Warfighting Acquisition System memorandum, the body of the FY2026 defense authorization act beyond its table of contents, and the defense portal for SBIR topics, which forbids automated reading [1238] [1263] [806] [52]. Where that is so, the record says so. Nothing here is legal, financial or compliance advice, and nothing is written for any one firm; a reader with a specific question has the governing text one link away.
