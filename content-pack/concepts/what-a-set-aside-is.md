## What it is

A set-aside is a purchase that the contracting officer restricts to small businesses. The test is called the rule of two: the purchase is restricted when offers are reasonably expected from two or more small firms [384]. A firm claims the status by representing its size in writing with its initial offer [427].

Two dollar lines sort the rule. Since October 1, 2025 the micro-purchase threshold is $15,000 and the simplified acquisition threshold is $350,000 [417]. Between them a purchase is set aside unless the contracting officer finds no reasonable expectation of offers from two or more small businesses, and statute reserves the same band [384] [374]. Above $350,000 the purchase is set aside when offers are expected from at least two responsible small businesses and award will be at fair market prices [384].

Four certified programs sit beside the general set-aside: 8(a), HUBZone, service-disabled veteran-owned (SDVOSB) and women-owned (WOSB). Above the simplified acquisition threshold they are considered before a general small-business set-aside, with no order of precedence among them [429]. Each also allows a sole-source award, one made without competition below a dollar ceiling. The codified FAR puts that ceiling at $8.5 million for manufacturing industry codes and $5.5 million otherwise for 8(a), HUBZone and WOSB, and at $8.5 million and $5 million for SDVOSB [338] [349] [357] [368]. SBA's own rule still prints $7,000,000 and $4,500,000 for 8(a) [331].

Orders are a separate case. Under a multiple-award contract, setting an order aside is at the contracting officer's discretion [420] [431]. An SBA rule proposed in October 2024 would apply the rule of two to orders; no final rule was found [472]. A set-aside also carries a condition: on a services set-aside above the simplified acquisition threshold, the small prime may pay no more than 50 percent of what the Government pays it to subcontractors that are not similarly situated [422].

## The key idea in one picture

Draw a ladder with three rungs, read upward by purchase value. Rung one, up to $15,000, is the micro-purchase, where the purchase card is the preferred method [417] [440]. Rung two, above $15,000 and up to $350,000, is reserved for small business unless two competitive offers cannot be expected [384].

Rung three, above $350,000 [417], holds two diamonds. The first asks whether one of the four certified programs fits; the second asks whether two responsible small firms are expected at fair market prices. A yes at either leads to a set-aside box; two noes lead to a box marked *not set aside* [429] [384]. A side rail labelled *sole source* runs beside rungs two and three and stops at each program's ceiling [338] [368]. A smaller ladder for orders under multiple-award contracts carries one label, *discretionary* [420].

## The maths, gently

$$ \$15{,}000 < V \le \$350{,}000 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{set aside unless } N < 2 $$

$$ V > \$350{,}000 \text{ and } N \ge 2 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{set aside} $$

- *V* is the expected value of the purchase and *N* is the number of small firms the contracting officer reasonably expects to offer [384] [417].
- Sole source outside manufacturing: the FAR line for 8(a) is $5.5 million and the line printed in 13 CFR 124.506 is $4,500,000, so an illustrative $5,000,000 requirement falls under one official text and over the other [338] [331].
- Limitation on subcontracting, services: on an illustrative $400,000 set-aside, at most 0.50 × $400,000 = $200,000 may go to subcontractors that are not similarly situated [422] [423].

## How this guide uses it

The route records for the total small-business set-aside, the partial set-aside, the order set-aside and the four program routes all rest on this idea. So do the gates for representing size, the limitations on subcontracting and the nonmanufacturer rule. Chapter 2 places the rule of two in the buying sequence and chapter 4 covers the certifications.
